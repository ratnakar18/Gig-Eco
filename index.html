Exhibit theme
The legal invisibility of India's gig workers

The object I picked for this fortnight is a technical report called "There's No Ghost in the Machine," published by the Centre for Labour Studies at the National Law School of India University together with IT for Change, based on interviews with 26 workers and five union organisers across Indian cities between November 2024 and February 2025. What struck me most is a quote from an Amazon warehouse worker in Manesar, who said people scanning products have a target of 1,200 products an hour while also having to load the truck at the same time, and that the pressure simply does not account for having to do both jobs together. If you damage a product while rushing to hit that number and the CCTV catches it, you get blacklisted. The report also documents Urban Company's auto acceptance feature, where the algorithm assigns you a job automatically and you cannot say no. It sounds efficient until a worker explains that the only cancellation reason the system accepts is an accident, so even being sick does not really count unless you can produce a doctor's note, and only for that one day. What makes this report matter for my exhibit on the legal invisibility of India's gig workers is the accountability gap it keeps surfacing. Workers can describe the algorithm's logic in surprising detail, comparing it to riding a bicycle where the manager holds the only speedometer, but knowing how you are being scored does not actually help you, because the same company that built the scoring system also runs the only complaint channel you are allowed to use. One worker straight up said that channel rarely works in their favour. This connects to something Michael Sandel argues in Justice: What's the Right Thing to Do?, which we read for Session 16, that how a system decides who gets a voice and who gets information is itself a moral choice and not a neutral technical detail. A grievance process that exists on paper but has no real teeth is not an accident. It is a design decision about who gets to feel heard, and this report suggests the psychological weight of that gap, described elsewhere as learned helplessness, falls entirely on the worker. The report also points out that Rajasthan's platform worker law does not even mention algorithmic decision making, while Karnataka, Telangana, and Jharkhand only require platforms to reveal some information about their systems, without ever giving a worker or a union the actual right to challenge whether an algorithm's decision was fair. That is the same gap Sandel is describing: transparency without any real justice attached to it. The Code on Social Security tells us gig workers sit in a legal grey zone. This report tells us what living inside that grey zone actually feels like on an ordinary working day, constantly watched, rarely heard, and never quite sure where the actual power sits.
