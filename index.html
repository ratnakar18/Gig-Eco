    Neither Employee
    Nor Free

    India's law has finally noticed its gig workers. It still won't say who they work for. This exhibit follows that gap right into a worker's head, one shift at a time, and cites everything it says.

    Our objective

    The law learned their name and stopped there

    India's Code on Social Security does give gig workers a name. It doesn't give them what employees get, like leave, insurance and a manager you can actually complain about. It doesn't give them what people running their own business get either, which is a say over their prices and their customers.

    I try to show that gap with evidence, then follow it to one cost you won't find in any statute: what happens to your peace of mind when an algorithm is your manager and the only complaint channel belongs to the company.

    2. Scroll through the evidence: thirteen sources, one slide each, grouped under four arguments.
    3. Spend a few minutes in the gallery, where posters and films give the numbers some faces.

    Findings of the report

    26workers interviewed for the report
    5union organisers interviewed
    1,200products an hour, the scanning target a warehouse worker describes

    My exhibit is about how India's gig workers are named by the law but left out of its protections. I wanted to look at one cost of that gap: what constant algorithmic management does to a worker's peace of mind.

    The object I picked is a technical report called "There's No Ghost in the Machine," written by Sadhana Sanjay, Nandini Chami, Saloni Mishra and Madhulika T. and published by the Centre for Labour Studies at the National Law School of India University, with IT for Change. It draws on interviews the authors held with 26 workers and five union organisers between November 2024 and February 2025.

    The title is the argument. We like to imagine the algorithm as a boss-bot that nobody controls. The authors show it is really a mix of software, platform executives and human managers, which means nobody gets to hide behind the machine.

    The details are hard to read. An Amazon worker in Manesar says scanners must hit 1,200 products an hour while the worker also loads the truck, and the target does not account for that. Another says even the millisecond it takes to move your phone from one hand to the other is logged as idle time. At Urban Company, auto acceptance hands you jobs without letting you refuse, and the only cancellation the system accepts without a penalty is an accident.

    The finding I find most useful is the second one, algorithmic anxiety. Workers can partly decode how the algorithm treats them. One even compared it to riding a bike where the company holds the only meter. But they cannot challenge any of it, because the same company runs the only complaint channel, and one worker said it rarely works in their favour. That gap between understanding and power is where the unease lives. Knowing the rules of a game you can never play differently does not calm anyone down. It just turns every shift into an exam you cannot pass.

    This is where Sandel's argument in Justice: What's the Right Thing to Do? helps. He asks whether a market deal is truly voluntary when the bargaining conditions are unequal. Auto acceptance is sold as a choice that pays more, but the price is losing your breaks, and a union organiser in the report notes that women with care responsibilities struggle to opt in and so earn less. A choice made with ratings, penalties and ID blocks hanging over you is not fully free.

    The legal side matters too. Rajasthan's law ignores algorithmic decisions entirely. Karnataka's Act and the proposed Telangana and Jharkhand laws give workers only a limited right to request information, with no way to challenge a decision and no rights for unions. The Code on Social Security tells us gig workers sit in a legal grey zone. This report shows what that grey zone feels like on an ordinary working day: constantly watched, rarely heard, and never sure where the power sits.
